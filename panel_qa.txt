  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "You say you enforce epsilon less than or equal to 1. How exactly
  do you track the cumulative privacy budget across 100 rounds?
  What accountant mechanism are you using?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "We use the Rényi Differential Privacy accountant — also called
  the RDP accountant — implemented via the Opacus library from
  Facebook Research.

  Standard DP composition theorems are too pessimistic — they assume
  the worst case at each step, leading to a very loose bound on the
  cumulative epsilon. The RDP accountant is tighter. It tracks the
  Rényi divergence at each training step and converts to standard
  epsilon-delta DP at the end using the Mironov 2017 conversion.

  In our setup — sigma equals 1.1, clipping norm C equals 1.0,
  delta equals 10 to the power of negative 5 — each local round
  consumes approximately epsilon 0.8 in the per-round Rényi sense.
  Over 100 rounds with composition, the cumulative epsilon is bounded
  and tracked in real time by the Opacus PrivacyEngine object.

  The PrivacyEngine is attached to the optimizer on each device —
  it automatically adds the calibrated Gaussian noise per mini-batch
  and increments the epsilon counter after each step."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Names the accountant precisely — RDP / Rényi
  ✓ Explains WHY RDP over basic composition — shows understanding
  ✓ Gives exact hyperparameters — sigma, C, delta
  ✓ Mentions Opacus — shows real implementation knowledge
  ✓ Explains how noise is added in practice

  │ Q5  [L3]  E2 — CMGA Technical                                    │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "Explain the CMGA mechanism mathematically. What exactly is the
  gate, what are its inputs, and what is the fused output?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "CMGA — Cross-Modal Gated Attention — operates at the edge server
  after receiving gradient flows from both encoders.

  The ECG encoder on each device produces a feature vector
  f_ecg in R-256. The X-ray encoder produces f_xray in R-256.
  Both vectors are gradient representations — not raw features —
  so they respect the differential privacy constraint.

  At the edge server, the gate is computed as:
  g = sigma( W_g · concatenate(f_ecg, f_xray) )

  where sigma is the sigmoid function and W_g is a learned weight
  matrix of dimension 256 by 512.

  The gate g is a vector in R-256, with values between 0 and 1.
  It represents how much weight to give the ECG stream at each
  feature dimension.

  The fused output is:
  f_fused = g element-wise-multiply f_ecg
            plus (1 minus g) element-wise-multiply f_xray

  This is an element-wise soft selection —
  where g is close to 1, the ECG gradient dominates;
  where g is close to 0, the X-ray gradient dominates.

  The fused gradient f_fused is then Top-k sparsified at k equals 10
  percent before being sent to the cloud — so only the top 10 percent
  of the 256 dimensions by absolute magnitude are transmitted."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ States input dimensions precisely — R-256
  ✓ Writes the gate equation correctly
  ✓ Explains the sigmoid gating behaviour intuitively
  ✓ States the fusion formula with element-wise operations
  ✓ Connects back to Top-k sparsification downstream

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Q6  [L3]  E1 — FedAvg vs ASWA                                    │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "You say ASWA replaces static FedAvg. FedAvg uses dataset size
  for weighting — n_k divided by N. What exactly does ASWA use
  instead, and why is that better?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "In standard FedAvg, the global model is computed as:
  W_global = sum over k of (n_k divided by N) multiplied by W_k

  where n_k is the number of samples on edge cluster k and N is
  the total. This is purely size-based weighting.

  The problem is that in a real healthcare deployment, a cluster
  with more samples is not necessarily a better contributor.
  A cluster whose patients all belong to one diagnostic class —
  say, all Normal ECGs — will push the global model toward that class,
  causing drift on minority classes like MI or STTC.

  ASWA replaces the static n_k divided by N weight with a dynamic
  quality score alpha_k, computed from two signals:

  First — the class entropy of the cluster's local data distribution.
  A cluster with high entropy — diverse class distribution — receives
  higher weight. A cluster dominated by one class gets lower weight.
  This discourages majority-class bias.

  Second — the packet delivery ratio of that cluster's edge link,
  measured by NS-3 in simulation. A cluster with poor connectivity —
  frequent packet loss — receives lower weight because its gradient
  updates are less reliable.

  So: W_global = sum over k of alpha_k multiplied by W_k

  where alpha_k balances data quality and network reliability.
  This outperforms FedAvg on non-IID data because it actively
  corrects for the two most common sources of model drift in
  real healthcare deployments."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ States the FedAvg formula first — shows you know the baseline
  ✓ Identifies the specific weakness — size weighting ignores quality
  ✓ Explains both components of alpha_k precisely
  ✓ Links class entropy to non-IID correction
  ✓ Links packet delivery ratio to network reliability

  │ Q8  [L4]  E1 — Killer — Gradient Leakage                         │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "You claim differential privacy protects patient data. But recent
  papers — Zhu et al. 2019, Geiping et al. 2020 — have shown that
  gradients can be INVERTED to reconstruct the original training image
  with high fidelity, even with noise. How does your framework
  prevent gradient inversion attacks?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "That is an excellent and important challenge — and those papers
  are correct. Naive gradient transmission IS vulnerable to inversion.

  Our framework addresses this at three levels.

  FIRST — Differential Privacy. DP-SGD adds calibrated Gaussian noise
  to every gradient before transmission. At epsilon equals 0.8 and
  sigma equals 1.1, the signal-to-noise ratio of the gradient is
  reduced to the point where reconstruction becomes statistically
  unreliable. This is the primary defence.

  SECOND — Top-k sparsification at k equals 10 percent. By transmitting
  only the top 10 percent of gradients by magnitude, we remove 90
  percent of the gradient information from the uplink channel.
  Inversion attacks require dense, high-fidelity gradients —
  sparse transmissions significantly degrade their effectiveness.

  THIRD — Hierarchical aggregation itself. The CMGA edge fusion
  aggregates gradients from multiple devices before they reach the
  cloud. An attacker at the cloud level receives a FUSED gradient —
  not an individual device's gradient. Reconstructing a single
  patient's data from an aggregated multi-device gradient is
  computationally infeasible.

  In Phase 5, we will formally measure this using a Membership
  Inference Attack — MIA — baseline. Our target is less than 5 percent
  MIA success rate at epsilon equals 0.8, which would demonstrate
  that the framework resists gradient inversion in practice."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Validates the question — does not dismiss Zhu or Geiping papers
  ✓ Three-level defence: DP noise, sparsification, aggregation
  ✓ Explains WHY each layer defends — not just listing them
  ✓ Mentions MIA metric — shows empirical validation planned
  ✓ Specific: epsilon, sigma, k — no vague claims

  │ Q9  [L3]  E1 — Novelty Challenge                                 │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "Cross-modal attention is not new. Transformers have been using
  cross-attention since 2017. How is your CMGA actually novel?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "You are absolutely right — cross-modal attention as a concept
  is well established in the transformer literature since Vaswani 2017.

  The novelty of CMGA is not the attention mechanism itself.
  The novelty is WHERE it operates and WHAT it processes.

  In standard multimodal transformers, cross-attention operates on
  raw feature embeddings — pixel patches, token embeddings — before
  any privacy constraint is applied. The model sees the actual data.

  CMGA operates on GRADIENT REPRESENTATIONS at the edge tier —
  after DP-SGD has already been applied on-device. So the inputs
  to the attention gate are not features — they are noisy, clipped,
  sparsified gradient vectors in a differentially private space.

  To the best of our knowledge, no published work applies a gated
  attention fusion mechanism to differentially private gradient
  streams within a hierarchical federated learning topology.

  The technical challenge CMGA solves is cross-modal gradient fusion
  without violating the per-device privacy budget — because the
  fusion happens AFTER noise injection, not before.
  Standard cross-modal attention would require the encoders to share
  intermediate feature activations — which would leak information
  across devices and violate the federated assumption."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Concedes the point immediately — does not fight the examiner
  ✓ Reframes novelty precisely: WHERE and WHAT — not the mechanism
  ✓ Identifies the specific technical distinction: gradient space vs feature space
  ✓ Uses "To the best of our knowledge" — not "we are the first"
  ✓ Explains the problem it solves that standard attention cannot

  │ Q10  [L2]  E2 — Novelty — SADP                                   │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "Per-layer differential privacy is not new either. Papers like
  Yu et al. 2021 have explored per-layer clipping. What is new
  in your SADP?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "You are correct that per-layer clipping has been explored —
  specifically in the context of natural language processing models
  like BERT, where later layers are more sensitive to label leakage.

  The distinction in SADP is the sensitivity score itself.

  In Yu et al., per-layer clipping norms are set based on
  GRADIENT NORM statistics from a clean, centralised pre-training run.
  That is not feasible in a federated setting — there is no
  centralised run to calibrate from, and using one would itself
  violate the privacy constraint.

  SADP computes the per-layer sensitivity score dynamically
  and locally on each device — using only the device's own
  gradient history across rounds. No cross-device information
  is shared to compute the sensitivity. Each device independently
  assigns sigma_l based on its own gradient norm trajectory.

  This makes SADP compatible with the federated, privacy-preserving
  constraint — which prior per-layer DP methods are not."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Acknowledges the prior work by name — not dismissive
  ✓ Identifies the specific gap: calibration requires centralised data
  ✓ Explains SADP's local, per-device computation approach
  ✓ Connects back to the federated constraint — the binding condition

  EXAMINER SAYS:

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "Alpha equals 0.5 is the standard non-IID partition parameter
  used in FedProx, SCAFFOLD, and MOON — the canonical federated
  learning papers for non-IID evaluation. At alpha equals 0.5,
  each client receives a skewed but overlapping class distribution —
  realistic for a multi-hospital scenario where different hospitals
  see different patient demographics but not completely disjoint ones.

  As alpha approaches zero, the distribution becomes increasingly
  extreme — in the limit, each client sees ONLY one class. This is
  called the pathological non-IID setting.

  Our framework is designed to handle this via two mechanisms.
  ASWA down-weights clusters with low class entropy — so an extreme
  non-IID cluster contributes less to the global aggregation.
  pFAL allows each device to maintain a local adapter that compensates
  for the global model's under-representation of its local class.

  We have not yet run experiments at alpha approaching zero —
  that is in our Phase 6 ablation study plan. However, we do not
  claim strong performance in the pathological non-IID case.
  At alpha equals 0.5, our framework is designed and targeted.
  Extreme non-IID performance is an open question I acknowledge."

  "You mentioned HIPAA, GDPR, and India's DPDP Act. Can you explain

  addresses and how?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 —
  the DPDP Act — has two provisions most directly relevant to
  our framework.

  Section 4 mandates data minimisation — personal data should be
  collected and processed only to the extent necessary for the
  stated purpose. Our framework satisfies this by transmitting
  ONLY gradient updates — mathematical derivatives of model weights —
  never the raw clinical data. The gradient is the minimum
  information needed for collaborative model training.

  Section 8 mandates purpose limitation — data must be used
  only for the purpose for which it was collected.
  In our framework, the differential privacy mechanism at epsilon
  equals 0.8 provides a mathematical guarantee that no information
  about a specific individual can be inferred from the gradient —
  preventing the model from being misused to identify patients.

  HIPAA's Safe Harbor provision is satisfied similarly —
  gradient-only transmission with DP noise means no Protected
  Health Information is transmitted at any point in the pipeline.

  GDPR Article 25 — privacy by design — is satisfied because
  DP-SGD is applied ON-DEVICE before any transmission occurs.
  Privacy is not added as an afterthought — it is the first
  operation in the training pipeline."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Names specific sections — Section 4 and Section 8 of DPDP
  ✓ Explains data minimisation concretely — gradients not raw data
  ✓ Connects epsilon budget to purpose limitation mathematically
  ✓ Addresses all three regulations — HIPAA, GDPR, DPDP
  ✓ Shows privacy by design is architectural — not bolt-on

  EXAMINER SAYS:
  "Every system has a fundamental weakness. What is the single biggest
  weakness of PHANTOM-FL that you have not yet resolved,
  and what would you do about it if you had another six months?"

  ── YOUR MODEL ANSWER ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  "The single biggest unresolved weakness is the CMGA mechanism's
  dependency on SIMULTANEOUS availability of both modalities.

  CMGA fuses ECG and X-ray gradients at the edge server.
  But in a real hospital cluster, an ECG patch and an X-ray unit
  do not always produce data at the same time for the same patient.
  A patient may have an ECG recording but no corresponding X-ray
  from the same visit — or vice versa.

  In the current implementation, if one modality is missing,
  the system falls back to unimodal training on that cluster.
  The CMGA gate receives a zero vector for the missing modality.
  This degrades the cross-modal fusion benefit for that round.

  What I would do with six additional months —

  I would replace the hard zero-masking fallback with an
  asynchronous cross-modal distillation approach.
  Specifically — when X-ray data is available but ECG is not,
  the X-ray encoder would generate a PSEUDO-ECG gradient
  via a lightweight cross-modal prediction head trained on
  paired data. This pseudo-gradient would fill the missing branch
  in CMGA, preserving fusion quality even in the asynchronous case.

  This is related to cross-modal knowledge distillation —
  a well-studied problem — and the ClinicalCMGA module we have
  designed in our IP-3 specification is the first step toward this."

  ── WHAT MAKES THIS ANSWER PASS ────────────────────────────────────
  ✓ Identifies a REAL weakness — not a trivial one
  ✓ Explains the mechanism of failure precisely
  ✓ Does not say "there are no weaknesses" — that kills credibility
  ✓ Proposes a concrete, technically grounded solution
  ✓ References the ClinicalCMGA IP-3 design — shows depth
