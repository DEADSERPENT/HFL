\begin{abstract}
Multimodal Internet-of-Things deployments in clinical settings generate
heterogeneous data streams that cannot be centralised, because the records are
governed by consent and by data-protection regimes that prohibit pooling.
Federated learning removes that obstacle by communicating model updates rather
than records, and a hierarchical organisation relieves the wide-area link that
otherwise constrains it; neither, however, supplies a privacy guarantee, and the
guarantee such systems do report is conventionally stated over training records
while the guarantee a clinical setting requires concerns individuals. Where a
single episode of care yields many records, the two differ for every
participant. This paper presents a three-tier hierarchical federated framework
in which the privacy unit is the patient by construction: gradients are grouped
by individual before they are bounded, the noise scale is calibrated against
every client simultaneously so that the interposition of the edge tier is
reflected in the reported budget, and the cost of releasing a conformal
calibration quantile is composed into that budget rather than omitted from it.
Representation learning is separated from private adaptation, with encoders
pretrained on a population disjoint from every client and frozen, so that only a
compact fusion head is privatised. The framework is evaluated on a cohort of
surgical cases in which both modalities originate from the same patient during
the same operation, and the resulting guarantee is audited by mounting
membership inference at the level of the individual, with every attack reported
alongside a positive control on a configuration known to memorise. A further
experiment shows that the price of the individual-level guarantee is not a fixed
quantity but a function of how many records each individual contributes,
vanishing when that number is one --- where the two units coincide by
construction, so that the experiment controls itself --- and growing
monotonically thereafter.
