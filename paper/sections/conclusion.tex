\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper addressed a mismatch between what private hierarchical federated
learning guarantees and what clinical IoT requires: existing systems protect
individual records, whereas a clinical deployment must protect patients, each
of whom contributes many records. It presented a hierarchical federated
learning system for multimodal clinical and wearable sensing that enforces
differential privacy at the level of the individual rather than the record,
and quantified the cost of that choice. On three deployments the training budget is $0.900$ on both clinical
deployments and $0.766$ on the wearable deployment, where the guarantee is
record-level; the guarantee is proved for the training transcript and holds
end to end only when the encoders and standardization constants are fitted on
public data, which among the evaluated configurations is the external clinical
one, run on a partition in which every individual is held by one hospital. The
deployments therefore carry different evidence: the external corpus
tests the patient-level guarantee end to end,
the first clinical corpus tests
it on the training transcript, and the wearable corpus evaluates the system at
record level. The guarantee
costs no utility on the first clinical deployment, partitioned by person, and $3.1$ points
of area under the ROC curve $[1.6, 4.7]$ on the external one, inconclusive
against the two-point target, and $3.9$ $[2.6, 5.2]$ end to end, which misses
it. Uplink traffic is reduced
by $90.8\,\%$, $96.1\,\%$ and $92.3\,\%$, modeled energy per round by
$72.3\,\%$, $76.8\,\%$ and $79.6\,\%$, and the 95th-percentile inference
latency on a workstation CPU is $2.3$, $0.75$ and $0.24$~ms against a requirement of $100$~ms. At $187$
clients on two edges, update delivery reaches $97.83\,\%$, below the $99\,\%$
target, because contention on the shared medium limits every configuration;
with eight edges the target is met in every round. Because noise is added to gradients during training,
the privacy mechanism adds no computation at inference.

Two findings are central. The first is that, on the clinical and wearable corpora, robustness to a tight
privacy budget is determined by the initialization rather than by the size of the privatized
subset. A pretrained initialization improves on a random one by $0.0129$
without noise and by $0.1991$ at the tightest budget, whereas under privacy
restricting the privatized subset changes the result by at most $0.0059$, with
intervals covering zero,
despite privatizing $3.3$ times fewer parameters. The restriction is therefore
justified by its communication savings only.

The second is that the cost of an individual-level guarantee increases with the number of
records each individual contributes. At a fixed per-step budget and number of
units per step, the penalty is indistinguishable from zero at one record per
individual and reaches $0.0872$ in average precision at eight, following the
one-parameter shape $A(1 - 1/k)$ motivated by Proposition~\ref{prop:scaling}. Because the two units coincide at one record per
individual, this configuration serves as a built-in control. The
individual-level arm remains flat while the record-level arm improves, which is
consistent with additional records that the stricter unit cannot exploit.
On the external corpus, normalizing the clipping bound by the record count
does not reduce this cost. On a second clinical corpus of $132{,}736$ individuals across $187$
hospitals, the ordering and the saturation transfer, whereas the magnitude is
approximately one ninth of the pre-registered forecast and is not resolved at
three seeds.

On VitalDB, a patient-level audit with a positive control is consistent with
the unit mattering: record-level training remains detectably above its
difficulty floor under offline LiRA (AUC $0.576$), whereas patient-level
training does not ($0.503$), a difference of $0.073$ $[0.054, 0.092]$. Two of
four attacks resolve the difference, and the audit bounds these attacks
without establishing the absence of leakage.

Several results are negative. Compression does not affect accuracy and is
justified by traffic reduction alone. The benefit of multimodal fusion is
conditional: it is $0.0370$ in area under the ROC curve on the first clinical
corpus, untestable on the wearable corpus, where the better single modality
reaches $0.9976$, and absent on the external clinical corpus. A titration on
the external corpus shows that balance between the two streams is a sufficient
condition: when the stronger stream is degraded to parity with the weaker,
fusion yields a significant gain in area under the ROC curve over the better
single modality, although not in average precision and not relative to the
intact stronger stream.

Several directions remain open. First, a third corpus is needed to
establish whether the saturation level of the scaling relationship is a constant of the
guarantee or a fixed fraction of the precision attainable on a task. Second,
on eICU the cost is set by the smallest hospitals, whose high sampling rates
force the largest noise multipliers; sampling patients across the clients of
an edge is the most direct route to meeting the utility target there. Third,
clipping each patient's gradient around its own mean, which the analysis
indicates could recover part of the unit's cost, should be tested. Finally, the
audit should be extended to eICU and, with more than sixteen shadow models and
a larger non-member set, to the low-false-positive regime.
