2  —  Why the Hypothesis Failed, and How to Get It Back

2.1 What we predicted

If you protect only records, an attacker should be able to tell whether a patient was in the training set. We predicted an attack success of about 0.70 against record-level DP and about 0.50 (chance) against patient-level DP. A gap of roughly 0.20.

2.2 What we measured

Record-level DP: 0.5477. Patient-level DP: 0.5224. Gap: +0.0325, and the confidence interval includes zero. All four attack variants gave the same answer.

2.3 Why it failed — the real reason

Not because the attack was weak. We proved that first: the positive control, on a model known to memorise, reached 0.6277 — it cleared the gate we set before running.

The honest reason is this. DP-SGD at ε = 1 is already very strong. The noise (σ = 11.33) is large enough that even record-level DP leaves almost no membership signal to find. The theoretical gap between record-level and patient-level protection is real, but at this budget both arms are pushed so close to chance that the difference between them is smaller than we can measure with 5 seeds.

A supporting clue confirms this. On the patient arm, LiRA actually performed worse than a plain loss threshold (−0.0071). That is what a defence with nothing left to leak looks like — not a failing attack.

Say it this way, always The large predicted effect is refuted. A small effect is not excluded — the interval is ±0.060 wide at n = 5. Never write “there is no effect”.

2.4 What we would need to get it back

#What to changeWhy it would helpCost
1Attack at a weaker privacy budget — ε = 4, 8, 16 instead of 1At ε = 1 both arms are near chance, so there is no room for a gap. Weaker DP leaves signal, and the record-vs-patient difference has space to show. This is the most likely route.Low
2Increase seeds from 5 to 20–30The interval half-width is ±0.060. The measured gap is +0.0325. More seeds would resolve whether that small gap is real.Medium
3Use a dataset with higher kVitalDB gives about 8 windows per patient. The more records a patient contributes, the bigger the theoretical gap. A dataset with 50–100 records per patient should show it more clearly.Needs eICU
4Attack the worst-case patient, not the averageAverage AUC hides the tail. Report TPR at 0.1 % FPR — the confident identifications that are the real clinical disclosure. The machinery already computes this.Low
5Attack the head-only recipe directlyWe already know head-only training memorises harder (0.6277 vs 0.5122). That is where leakage lives. This is the finding that survived.Done

Route 1 is the recommendation. The hypothesis may not be wrong — it may simply have been tested at a budget where nothing is visible.

6  —  What Does Not Exist Anywhere Else

This is the list of things we could not find in any paper we studied. It is the answer to “what is new?”

#Does not exist in the literatureClosest existing work, and what it stops short of
1The privacy cost measured as a curve in records-per-individual.Nobody has done this. User-level DP papers (McMahan 2018) report one number for one dataset. The controlling variable has never been isolated.
2A hierarchical FL system that carries a formal patient-level guarantee.HED-FL, HCEF and Mhaisen et al. are hierarchical with no privacy guarantee at all. FL2DP and RoPPFL have privacy but are not hierarchical. The two lines have never met.
3Conformal calibration cost composed into the privacy budget.Conformal FL papers release the calibration quantile for free. Smith (2011) gives the DP quantile mechanism, but nobody charges it to the same budget as training. We do: 0.7705 + 0.2000.
4Patient-level membership inference audit with a passing positive control.MIA audits in FL are conducted at record level — the quantity a record-level guarantee already protects, so the least informative test. Almost none report a positive control, so their nulls cannot be trusted.
5The finding that parameter-efficient DP fine-tuning increases memorisation.Li et al. (2021) and Yu et al. (2021) propose head-only DP fine-tuning for efficiency. Neither measures whether it leaks more. It does.
6Noise calibrated against every client simultaneously in a hierarchy.Standard practice calibrates against a representative client. That silently understates the budget when an edge tier changes how often each client is sampled. The failure is invisible in a training log.
7DP shifting attention toward missingness.Not reported anywhere. Missingness-as-signal exists in clinical ML, but its interaction with privacy noise does not.
8A genuinely paired multimodal clinical FL benchmark under patient-level DP.Multimodal FL papers pair modalities by construction rule or use unpaired corpora. Ours are paired by the recording apparatus — same patient, same second.

Honest boundary: items 2–8 are measurements and practices, not new mathematics. The mechanisms themselves (user-level DP, parameter-efficient DP fine-tuning, DP quantile release) are prior art and are cited as such. Claiming to have invented them would be an immediate desk reject. Item 1 is the closest thing to a genuinely new scientific result.

8  —  Contribution of Multi-Data and Multi-Model

8.1 Multi-data — two datasets

Using two very different corpora is what let us separate system claims from task claims.

  • VitalDB (clinical, 6,257 patients, ~8 windows each) carries every privacy claim. It is the only one that can, because patient-level work needs many records per person.
  • WEAR (wearable, 22 participants) carries the systems claims and proves they are not an artefact of one topology or payload size.

The two agree on everything structural: uplink reduction, energy, latency, reliability and privacy budget all hold on both. That is the evidence that the architecture generalises.

They disagree on the task claims, and that disagreement is itself a finding: fusion helps on VitalDB (+0.0370 AUROC) and is pointless on WEAR (+0.0004, ceiling). We report this rather than hide it.

8.2 Multi-model — two modalities

Each dataset pairs two genuinely different signal types recorded from the same person at the same moment:

DatasetModality AModality BA onlyB onlyFusedGain
VitalDBIntraoperative signals (time series)Preoperative clinical + labs (static vector)0.82830.76800.8653+0.0370
WEARAccelerometer (time series)Egocentric video features0.99760.99570.9980+0.0004

AUROC. VitalDB fusion gain is 5.9× the seed standard deviation over 5 seeds. WEAR is at ceiling and cannot evidence the claim.

Why the multimodal design matters to the privacy question. This is the part that is easy to miss. Two modalities mean the model has two sources of evidence about the same patient. That makes the patient — not the window — the natural unit of both learning and disclosure. A single patient's identity is carried by the combination of their signals and their clinical record. Protecting one window of one modality protects almost nothing about that person.

So multimodality is not decoration here. It is the reason the unit-of-privacy question has teeth in the first place.

Honest limitation The fusion claim rests on one dataset. WEAR structurally cannot corroborate it. This is the second-strongest reason to add a third clinical dataset — the first being the k-law.

9  —  Why We Want PhysioNet Data (eICU-CRD)

9.1 The problem with what we have

Every privacy claim in this research rests on one dataset from one hospital. VitalDB comes from a single institution, and our nine “clients” are a partition we created ourselves. WEAR cannot help — it has 22 people, one client each, and no patient contributes enough records for the k-law to even be defined.

A reviewer will ask one question, and it is the right question:

“Is this a property of patient-level private federated learning,
or a property of your dataset?”

Right now we cannot answer it.

9.2 Why eICU-CRD specifically

Property of eICU-CRDWhy we need it
208 real hospitalsOur clients would be actual institutions, not a partition we invented. This is what federated learning is supposed to model, and we currently only simulate it.
Independent patient populationDifferent demographics, different care patterns, different equipment. If the k-law survives that, it is structural.
Same patient → many records structureThe k-law needs it. VitalDB gives about 8 records per patient; eICU gives far more, which stretches the curve into a range we cannot currently reach.
Same modality structure — time-series vitals plus static clinical variablesNo new encoder needed. The existing pipeline applies almost unchanged, so this is weeks of work, not months.
Credentialed access with ethics trainingReviewers trust PhysioNet. Access requires CITI training and a verified reference, which is itself evidence the work was conducted properly.
Widely used benchmarkResults on eICU can be compared against other published work. Results on a private partition cannot.

9.3 What we will do with it — and the one rule

We will not simply run the same experiment and add another table. That is worth little.

Instead: fit the k-curve on VitalDB, write down a numeric prediction of what the gap should be at eICU's k, freeze that prediction before touching the data, then test it.

Why the prediction must be frozen first The value of this experiment is that it could fail. If we fit the curve, look at eICU, and then write the prediction, no reviewer can tell that apart from retrofitting — and neither can we. This is why the current wait for access is useful time rather than lost time: the pre-registration has to be written now.

9.4 Both outcomes are publishable

If the prediction holds — the cost of patient-level privacy is structural, and we can give practitioners a rule: given your k, this is what ε = 1 will cost you. Nobody has published that.

If the prediction misses — we have found the boundary condition, with a dated forecast proving the story was not adjusted after the fact. That is also publishable, and visibly honest.

A paper reporting that two independent clinical datasets were tested, one confirming and one exposing a limit, is stronger than one where everything conveniently worked.
